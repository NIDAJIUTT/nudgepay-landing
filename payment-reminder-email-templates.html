Payment Reminder Email Templates That Actually Get Paid

Updated October 20, 2026 · By the NudgePay team

Most late payments aren't malice — they're inbox chaos. The freelancers who get paid on time aren't luckier; they send the right reminder at the right moment, with the right tone. Below are six copy-paste payment reminder email templates covering the full arc, from a friendly heads-up before the due date to a firm final notice at 30 days.

Why templates (and timing) matter

Writing a payment reminder from scratch is awkward, so most people delay it — and every day of delay makes the invoice easier to forget. A template removes the friction: you fill in three fields and send. The second advantage is tone control. A reminder written in frustration sounds accusatory; a template written once, calmly, stays professional even on day 30.

Timing matters just as much. Research and hard experience agree on the same shape: remind before the due date (most people pay faster when they see it coming), confirm on the day itself, then follow up at widening intervals with gradually firmer language. Each template below follows that ladder. Replace the bracketed fields — [Invoice #], [Amount], [Due date], [Client name], [Payment link] — and send.

The 6 templates

3 days before due — the friendly heads-up

Template 1: The pre-due heads-up

Subject: Quick heads-up — Invoice [Invoice #] due [Due date]

Hi [Client name],

Just a friendly heads-up that Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount] comes due on [Due date]. You can pay it here in about a minute: [Payment link]

If anything on the invoice looks off, just reply to this email and I'll sort it out right away. Otherwise there's nothing else you need to do — the link above takes care of everything.

Thanks again for the project,

[Your name]

Why it works: sent before there's any problem, it feels like customer service, not chasing. A large share of "late" payments are simply invoices that landed at the wrong moment — this email quietly fixes that.

On the due date — the gentle confirmation

Template 2: Due today

Subject: Invoice [Invoice #] ([Amount]) — due today

Hi [Client name],

A quick note that Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount] is due today. Here's the payment link if you need it: [Payment link]

If you've already sent payment, thank you — please ignore this message. If it's still on your list, today's the day it clears the books on my end.

Appreciate it,

[Your name]

Tone: neutral and brief. The "if you've already paid, ignore this" line matters — it keeps the email from feeling like an accusation and covers the very common case of a payment already in transit.

3 days late — the "did it slip through?" nudge

Template 3: Just past due

Subject: Past due — Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount]

Hi [Client name],

I hope you're doing well. I'm writing because Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount] was due on [Due date], and I haven't seen the payment come through yet — it may simply have slipped through the cracks.

You can pay it here: [Payment link]

If there's an issue with the invoice, or if payment is already on its way, just let me know either way.

Many thanks,

[Your name]

Tone: assume good intent. Most invoices at this stage are genuinely forgotten or stuck in an approval queue. Asking "is there an issue?" gives the client an easy way to surface a dispute before it becomes a standoff.

7 days late — the firm follow-up

Template 4: One week overdue

Subject: Invoice [Invoice #] is 7 days overdue — [Amount]

Hi [Client name],

Following up on Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount], which was due on [Due date]. It's now a week overdue, and since I haven't heard back, I wanted to check in directly.

If payment is already in progress, great — could you confirm when it was sent? If something is holding it up, please tell me today so we can get it resolved. You can pay here: [Payment link]

Best regards,

[Your name]

What's different: you now ask for a specific response ("confirm when it was sent" / "tell me today"). Silence stops being a free option. Keep it factual — no sarcasm, no guilt trips.

14 days late — consequences enter the chat

Template 5: Two weeks overdue

Subject: Action needed — Invoice [Invoice #] is 14 days overdue

Hi [Client name],

Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount] is now 14 days past its due date of [Due date], and I haven't received payment or a reply to my earlier messages.

I'd like to keep this straightforward: please pay by [New date] using this link: [Payment link]

If I don't hear from you by then, I'll need to pause work on [Project] and, per our agreement, a late fee may apply to the overdue balance.

I value our working relationship, and I'm confident we can close this out quickly.

Regards,

[Your name]

Tone: still courteous, but now there are dates and consequences. Only name consequences you will actually follow through on — an empty threat teaches the client to ignore the next one.

30 days late — the final notice

Template 6: Final notice

Subject: Final notice — Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount]

Hi [Client name],

This is a final notice regarding Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount], originally due on [Due date] and now 30 days overdue. Despite several reminders, payment has not been received.

Please arrange payment in full by [Deadline — 7 days from today] via this link: [Payment link]

If payment is not received by that date, I will need to consider my options for recovering the amount, which may include a formal demand and third-party collection.

If you have already paid, please send proof of payment so I can update my records.

[Your name]

Keep this one short and documentary. At this point the email is also a record — it may be read later by a mediator, a collections agency, or a judge. Write nothing you wouldn't want read aloud.

Three rules that make every template work better

One ask, one link. Every reminder should contain exactly one clear next step: pay via this link. Multiple asks ("pay, or call me, or reply with your timeline") diffuse responsibility.

Quote the facts. Invoice number, amount, due date — every time. Vague reminders ("about that invoice…") get ignored; specific ones get filed, forwarded, and paid.

Send from a thread. Reply to the original invoice email rather than starting a new one. The client sees the full history, and so does anyone they forward it to in accounts payable.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I send payment reminders?

A proven cadence is: a friendly heads-up 3 days before the due date, a short note on the due date itself, then follow-ups 3, 7, and 14 days after. If you reach 30 days, send a formal final notice. That is roughly one message a week — frequent enough to stay top of mind, spaced enough to stay professional. Sending daily reminders backfires: it trains clients to treat your emails as noise.

Can I charge late fees?

Usually yes — but only if the fee (or interest) was agreed in your contract, proposal, or invoice terms before the work started. A common structure is 1–1.5% per month on overdue balances. Rules vary by location and by whether your client is a business or a consumer, so state your terms clearly up front, apply them consistently, and check local regulations if you are unsure. A late fee you announce after the fact is hard to enforce; one in the signed terms is leverage.

What if they still ignore the final notice?

Escalate in writing: send a formal demand letter with a hard deadline, then weigh small claims court or a collections agency against the amount owed and the strength of your paper trail. Keep every email — that trail is your evidence. For small amounts, some freelancers write the debt off, keep the client on a permanent "prepay only" list, and move on. Either way, stop extending credit to chronic late payers.

Should I stop work for late payers?

For ongoing projects, yes — pausing work is your strongest and most professional lever, and it works best when your contract includes a pause clause. Announce it calmly in your 14-day reminder ("I'll need to pause work until the balance is settled"), then follow through without drama. Continuing to deliver while invoices age usually makes the balance — and the problem — bigger.
