Client Not Paying? The Exact Playbook to Get Your Invoice Paid

Updated October 20, 2026 · By the NudgePay team

An unpaid invoice sits in a strange emotional category: too small to lawyer up over, too big to shrug off. The mistake most freelancers and small businesses make is improvising — one apologetic email, two weeks of hoping, then an angry message they regret. What works instead is a calm, pre-planned escalation: six steps, each with a clear trigger, a clear message, and a clear consequence. Here it is.

A note before you start: run the steps in order, even if the invoice is already weeks old. Skipping straight to threats burns relationships and, paradoxically, makes you easier to ignore. Escalation works because each step is reasonable — and because the client can feel the floor rising.

1
Before you chase · Day 0

Verify the invoice actually landed

You would be surprised how many "non-paying clients" never received the invoice at all. It went to spam. It went to a project manager who left the company. It was sent to "accounts@" — an inbox nobody monitors. Before assuming bad faith, confirm delivery: check that the email didn't bounce, confirm you used the exact recipient your client specified (many companies require invoices to go to a specific AP address or portal), and re-send the invoice as a fresh email with the PDF attached, not buried in a thread.

This step also protects you later. If a dispute ever escalates, "I sent it once in March" is weak; "sent March 3, re-sent March 10 with confirmation" is a record. Ask for a one-line acknowledgment — it takes the client ten seconds and removes their most common excuse.

What to write

"Hi [Name] — re-sending Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount], due [Due date], with the PDF attached. Could you confirm you've received it and that it's with the right person for payment? Thanks!"
2
Days 1–3 overdue

Send the friendly nudge

The first overdue message should be almost aggressively pleasant. Assume the most boring explanation — a busy week, an approver on holiday, a payment run that happens monthly — because it's usually true. Your goal at this stage is information, not pressure: you want either payment or an explanation, and both are wins.

Keep it short, reference the invoice number and amount, include the payment link again, and end with an easy question. If the client replies with "sorry, paying Friday," you now have a commitment in writing — which makes every later step easier. If they don't reply at all, that silence is itself information: move to Step 3 on schedule rather than sending three more friendly nudges.

The key line

"Just flagging that Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount] was due on [Due date] — here's the payment link: [Payment link]. If it's already on its way, no action needed; if anything's blocking it, let me know and we'll sort it out."
3
Day 7 overdue

The firm follow-up — change the tone, not the temperature

At one week, drop the softeners. "Just checking in!" and "just wondering…" signal that your invoice is optional. Firm doesn't mean rude — it means specific: state the number of days overdue, restate the amount, and ask a direct question with a deadline for the reply. You're no longer asking whether there's a problem; you're asking when payment will be made.

If your contact is a champion inside the company rather than the payer, this is also the moment to gently widen the circle: ask who in accounts payable owns the invoice, and offer to re-send everything directly to them. Many "won't pay" situations are actually "can't pay until procurement has a PO number" situations — Step 3 is where you find out.

What to write

"Invoice [Invoice #] for [Amount] is now 7 days overdue. Could you confirm the payment date today? If there's an issue with the invoice itself, I need to know what it is so I can fix it this week."
4
Day 14 overdue

Create a paper trail

Two weeks overdue with no payment and no convincing explanation — now you write for the record. Send a single email that recaps everything: invoice number, amount, original due date, the dates of your previous reminders, and any promises made ("per your message on the 12th, payment was expected Friday"). Attach the invoice and, if you have one, the signed agreement or statement of work.

This isn't pedantry. A clean paper trail changes outcomes in three ways: it often gets forwarded internally with a "please just pay this" note; it's exactly what a collections agency or small claims filing will ask for; and it signals to the client that you've shifted from hoping to documenting. Keep the tone flat and factual — outrage undermines credibility, chronology builds it.

The key line

"For the record: Invoice [Invoice #] ([Amount], due [Due date]) remains unpaid. I've followed up on [date] and [date] and haven't yet received payment or a confirmed payment date."
5
Days 14–21 overdue

Pause work and apply leverage

If you're mid-project with this client, your unpaid time is funding their business. Now is when you use your two legitimate levers. First: pause work. Announce it professionally, tie it explicitly to the overdue balance, and state the restart condition — payment received. If your contract has a pause or suspension clause (it should), cite it. Second: apply the late fee or interest your terms provide for, and show the updated balance in writing. Fees announced in advance and applied calmly are business; fees invented in anger are arguments.

Expect this step to feel uncomfortable — that's normal, and it's why you announced it in earlier steps rather than springing it. Clients who intended to pay usually pay within days of a pause, because the cost of the delay has become visible to them. Clients who still don't move have told you something important about the relationship: believe them, and protect yourself accordingly.

What to write

"Until Invoice [Invoice #] is settled, I'm pausing work on [Project] effective today — happy to restart the same day payment lands. As per our terms, the balance including late fees is now [Updated amount]."
6
Day 30+ overdue

The formal demand — your last resort before outside help

At 30 days, after documented reminders and (usually) a work pause, send a formal demand: a short, unambiguous letter — email is fine — stating the amount owed, the work it covers, the full payment history, a final deadline (7–14 days is standard), and the specific next step you'll take if it passes. That next step might be small claims court, a collections agency, or reporting the debt, depending on the amount and your jurisdiction.

Two cautions. First, only name a consequence you will actually carry out; a demand letter that expires quietly teaches the client to wait out the next one too. Second, this guide is practical business advice, not legal advice — thresholds, interest rules, and filing procedures vary by place, and for larger sums a one-hour consultation with a local attorney pays for itself. Whatever you decide, send the demand, keep the receipt, and stop doing new work for this client until the slate is clean.

The key line

"Please consider this a formal demand for payment of [Amount] for Invoice [Invoice #]. If payment is not received by [Deadline], I will pursue recovery through the appropriate channels without further notice."

Prevention beats the playbook

Every step above gets easier — or unnecessary — with three habits set before work starts: a signed agreement with payment terms and a late-fee clause, a deposit or milestone billing on larger projects, and terms shorter than net-30 for new clients. Frequently asked questions

When should I worry about an unpaid invoice?

Start paying close attention at 7 days overdue, and treat 14 days plus silence as a genuine red flag. Most honest clients respond to an early nudge, even if only to explain a delay or ask for a few more days. The worrying pattern isn't lateness itself — businesses run late for mundane cash-flow reasons all the time — it's lateness combined with no reply. Silence is the signal to escalate on schedule.

Can I charge interest on late invoices?

Often yes, if interest or a late fee was written into your contract, proposal, or invoice terms before the work began — 1–1.5% per month is a common B2B structure. Some jurisdictions also grant statutory interest on commercial debts even without a clause. What you generally can't do is invent a charge after the fact and expect it to stick, so put the clause in every agreement from now on and reference it, calmly, from your second reminder onward.

How do I keep the relationship while chasing payment?

Separate the money conversation from the work conversation: stay warm about the project, stay factual about the invoice. Assume a benign explanation first, give the client an easy way to surface a problem, and never send a reminder written in anger — wait an hour, then edit. Most clients actually respect a supplier who follows up calmly and predictably. It signals that you're a professional with a process, not someone who can be safely ignored.

When is it time for a demand letter?

Around 30 days overdue, after at least three documented reminders have gone unanswered or promises have been broken. A demand letter states the amount, the deadline (usually 7–14 days), and what happens next — small claims, collections, or both. It's the last step before outside help gets involved, so send it when you mean it, and follow through if the deadline passes.
